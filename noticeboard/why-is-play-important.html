Why is play important?

› Noticeboard › Why is play important?

Community Playlink Team · Noticeboard

Watch a toddler stack blocks only to knock them down and laugh, or two children negotiate the rules of an invented game on the spot, and it’s tempting to file the behaviour under “just having fun.” But developmental researchers have spent decades showing that play is not a break from the serious business of growing up — it is the serious business of growing up. Play is how children build the cognitive, emotional, and social architecture they’ll rely on for the rest of their lives.

Play is how young brains build themselves

When a child pretends a cardboard box is a spaceship, or figures out why the tower keeps toppling on one side, they are running small experiments in cause and effect, symbolic thinking, and problem-solving. The American Academy of Pediatrics has described play as “not frivolous” but “brain building,” pointing to research linking play with the development of executive function — the set of skills that let children plan, focus attention, remember instructions, and juggle multiple tasks (AAP, The Power of Play). These are the same skills that later underpin reading comprehension, mathematical reasoning, and the ability to manage a busy school timetable.

Crucially, this isn’t limited to structured, adult-designed activities. A wide-ranging evidence review commissioned by the LEGO Foundation found that play — especially the open-ended, child-led kind — supports learning across physical, cognitive, social, and emotional domains simultaneously, precisely because it engages children in ways that feel meaningful and enjoyable to them rather than imposed from outside (Learning through Play: A Review of the Evidence). Children don’t compartmentalise a game of tag into “physical exercise” versus “social practice” versus “spatial reasoning” — it’s all happening at once, which is part of why play is such an efficient teacher.

Play teaches children how to be with other people

Some of the most important lessons children learn through play have nothing to do with academics. Pretend play and games with rules require children to take another person’s perspective, negotiate, compromise, and cope with disappointment when they don’t get their way. A child who wants to keep playing has to learn to share the crayons, agree on whose turn it is, and manage the frustration of losing a game without the whole thing falling apart. These are early, low-stakes rehearsals for the much higher-stakes social skills of adult life — collaboration, conflict resolution, empathy.

Play also gives children a safe way to process big feelings. A child who re-enacts a trip to the doctor with a stuffed animal, or plays “monster” and then decides who wins, is often working through fear, curiosity, or confusion in a form they can control. The AAP’s research on parent-child play highlights that shared play is also one of the most reliable ways parents build strong attachment and communication with their children, particularly in a child’s earliest years (AAP, The Importance of Play in Promoting Healthy Child Development).

Play builds resilience and self-regulation

Unstructured play, in particular, gives children practice at making their own decisions, setting their own goals, and solving problems without an adult stepping in to fix things. Deciding how to build a den that keeps collapsing, or working out the rules of a new game with friends, teaches children that setbacks are solvable rather than catastrophic. Psychologists who study play argue that this kind of self-directed activity is where children develop a sense of agency — the belief that they can act on the world and shape outcomes — which is closely tied to long-term mental health and resilience.

This is one reason the decline in unstructured outdoor play over recent decades has worried researchers and paediatricians alike: as more of children’s time has shifted toward structured, adult-supervised activities and screens, opportunities to practise this kind of independent problem-solving have narrowed. It’s also why play is treated as more than a nicety in international policy. The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child recognises play, rest, and recreation as a fundamental right of every child under Article 31 — not a privilege to be earned after chores and homework are done, but something children are entitled to as part of a healthy childhood (UNCRC Article 31).

What this means in practice

None of this means every moment of childhood needs to be “productive” play, or that parents need to engineer elaborate learning experiences. In fact, the research points the other way: some of the richest developmental benefits come from ordinary, low-pressure, child-led play — building a fort, inventing a game with siblings, drawing without a template to follow. Adults don’t need to run the show; they mostly need to protect the time and space for it to happen, whether that’s unscheduled hours after school, safe outdoor spaces, or simply resisting the urge to fill every gap in a child’s day with an organised activity.

Play looks like idleness from the outside. From the inside, it’s where children build the thinking skills, social skills, and emotional resilience that carry them into adulthood. That’s a strong case for treating play not as the reward for finishing the “real” work of childhood, but as some of the most important work a child does.

Sources

  • American Academy of Pediatrics, “The Power of Play: A Pediatric Role in Enhancing Development in Young Children,” Pediatrics (2018)
  • American Academy of Pediatrics, “The Importance of Play in Promoting Healthy Child Development and Maintaining Strong Parent-Child Bonds,” Pediatrics (2007)
  • LEGO Foundation, “Learning through Play: A Review of the Evidence”
  • International Play Association, “Child’s Right to Play: Article 31 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child”
